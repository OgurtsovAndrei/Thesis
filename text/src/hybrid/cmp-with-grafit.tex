Sections~\ref{sec:hybrid-scan} and~\ref{subsec:fpr-guarantee} have
already told us \emph{when} Seg-ARE is compact: when the input has
dense regions separated by gaps the forward sweep can isolate.
Below we work through two examples and count the bits.

Grafite, when the requested $\varepsilon$ falls below
the lossless threshold $u/n$, degenerates into a single Elias--Fano (EF) encoding of
the raw sorted keys --- the wrapper substitutes
\texttt{ef\_sux\_vector(keys)} and the filter answers range queries by
predecessor probe (Section~\ref{subsec:grafite-fallback}).
Below this threshold Grafite is an approximate filter; above it, it is
essentially EF over the whole universe.
Similar to Adaptive Filter~\ref{sec:adaptive}.

Seg-ARE applies the same EF primitive, but only after segmenting the
input.
Each dense cluster identified by the forward sweep is handed to
an exact-mode ERE sub-filter (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}), which
encodes the cluster's keys locally; sparse keys outside any cluster
fall through to the truncation hash (Section~\ref{sec:truncation}).
The total size of a Seg-ARE filter is the sum of per-cluster EF
encodings plus the fallback truncation filter --- versus Grafite's
single EF encoding over the whole universe.

This framing makes the win condition concrete: Seg-ARE beats Grafite
exactly when the per-cluster EF plus the fallback together cost less
than one universe-wide EF.
The bpk gap then decomposes as
\[
    \underbrace{\log_2(U_{\text{global}}/n)}_{\text{Grafite EF data}}
    \;-\;
    \underbrace{\Bigl(\sum_{c} \frac{n_c}{n} \cdot
        \log_2\!\bigl(2^{M_c}/n_c\bigr) \;+\; \frac{n_{\text{fb}}}{n} \cdot
        \log_2(1/\varepsilon)\Bigr)}_{\text{Seg-ARE: EF data + truncation fallback}},
\]
where $U_{\text{global}}$ is the spread of the whole input, $2^{M_c}$
and $n_c$ are the local window width and key count of the $c$-th
cluster, and $n_{\text{fb}}$ is the number of keys handed to the
fallback.

The two paragraphs below work through two examples: a synthetic
clustered distribution and the SOSD-Wikipedia timestamps.

\paragraph{Clustered synthetic ($n = 2^{28}$, build-$L = 1$,
$\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$).}
The clustered generator places keys across essentially the full
$2^{64}$ universe, so $\log_2(U/n) = 36$ bits per key for the
universe-wide baseline.

At build-time $\delta = 512 \cdot L / (2\varepsilon) = 25\,600$ the
forward sweep isolates $8911$ dense clusters covering $72.9\%$ of the
keys, with $M_c \in [14.6,\, 28.7]$ bits and
$n_c \in [512,\, 6.7\!\times\!10^{7}]$. The remaining $27.1\%$ fall
through to the truncation hash. The key-weighted per-cluster EF cost
is
\[
    \frac{1}{\sum_c n_c} \sum_c n_c \cdot \log_2\!\Bigl(\frac{2^{M_c}}{n_c}\Bigr)
    \;\approx\; 2.87 \text{ bits per in-cluster key}
\]
--- a $33$-bit reduction in EF \emph{data} cost relative to a
hypothetical universe-wide EF over all $2^{64}$.

Grafite cannot trigger its lossless fallback
(Section~\ref{subsec:grafite-fallback}) at the requested $\varepsilon$
either: lossless requires $n\mathcal{L}/\varepsilon > U$, i.e.\
$\varepsilon < n\mathcal{L}/U = 2^{28}\!\cdot\!128/2^{64} \approx
1.9\!\times\!10^{-9}$, far below the $10^{-2}$ we set. At any
$\varepsilon$ used in this evaluation Grafite stays in approximate
mode and follows the Goswami line $\log_2(\mathcal{L}/\varepsilon) +
O(1)$. Universe-wide lossless EF for this input would actually cost
$\log_2(U/n) + 2 + \text{select-index} \approx 38.94$~BPK
(measured at the saturating end of the Grafite sweep at requested
BPK ${\geq}40$) --- so high that lossless on a $2^{64}$ universe is
useless as a filter: it stores more bits per key than the keys
themselves carry information.

Figure~\ref{fig:seg-clustered-L128} shows the empirical curve at
query length $L = 128$. At matched
$\mathrm{FPR} \approx 6\!\times\!10^{-5}$, Seg-ARE sits at $9.77$ BPK
and Grafite at $20.63$ BPK. Both filters then drop below the
$4\!\times\!10^{-6}$ measurement floor (set by the $10^{6}$-query
batch): Seg-ARE at $10.07$ BPK ($K = 50$) and Grafite at
$26.63$ BPK (requested $\text{BPK}=26$); the true FPR at these points
is non-zero but below detection, not literally zero. The $16.5$-BPK
gap at the floor is therefore a lower bound on the BPK separation:
matching FPRs deeper would widen the gap further, with Grafite's
genuinely zero-FPR point only at the $38.94$~BPK lossless saturation.

The Seg-ARE per-key budget decomposes as
\[
\text{Seg-ARE BPK}\;\approx\;
\underbrace{0.729 \cdot (\,2.87 + 2.53)}_{\text{in-cluster: EF data + ERE metadata}} +
\underbrace{0.271 \cdot w_{\text{fb}}}_{\text{fallback truncation}}
\;=\;3.94 + (\text{fallback}),
\]
with fallback measured (by subtracting the in-cluster contribution
from the observed total) at $10.07 - 3.94 \approx 6.1$~BPK, i.e.\
${\sim}22.6$ bits per fallback key --- consistent with a truncation
filter at $K_{\text{fb}}$ tuned to the same $\varepsilon$ over the
$7.7\!\times\!10^{7}$ leftover keys. Grafite, by contrast, pays
$\log_2(128/6\!\times\!10^{-5}) + {\sim}2 \approx 23$ bits at
$\mathrm{FPR} = 6\!\times\!10^{-5}$, observed at $20.6$ (about $2$
bits below the worst-case constant).

\paragraph{SOSD-Wikipedia ($n = 2^{28}$, $L = 1$,
$\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$).}
Wikipedia edit timestamps form a tight near-sequential cluster:
after deduplication $n \approx 9 \times 10^{7}$ keys span a window
of width $\max - \min \approx 2.37\!\times\!10^{8}$ (about $28$
bits), giving $\log_2(U/n) \approx 1.4$ bits per key for a
universe-wide EF baseline. The segmenter does not collapse the
input into a single cluster: at $\delta = 25\,600$ the forward
sweep finds $359$ dense clusters covering $99.9\%$ of the keys,
with one giant cluster of $7.7\!\times\!10^{7}$ keys
($M_c = 26.5$ bits) and $358$ smaller ones ($M_c$ down to $14.6$
bits). The key-weighted per-cluster EF cost is
\[
    \frac{1}{\sum_c n_c} \sum_c n_c \cdot \log_2\!\Bigl(\frac{2^{M_c}}{n_c}\Bigr)
    \;\approx\; 0.62 \text{ bits per in-cluster key},
\]
$0.77$ bits per key below the universe-wide $1.4$.

Empirically (Figure~\ref{fig:seg-wiki-L128}), Grafite reaches
$\mathrm{FPR} = 0$ at $4.6$ BPK and Seg-ARE at $3.74$ BPK --- a
$0.9$-BPK gap. This matches the segmentation prediction almost
exactly: the $0.77$-bit-per-key drop in EF data cost applied to
the $99.9\%$ in-cluster fraction gives $0.77$ BPK, leaving
${\sim}0.1$ BPK to small implementation constants.

The view also predicts the failure modes: when the input has no
isolable dense regions (uniform, OSM), every key falls through to the
fallback, the per-cluster sum collapses to a single truncation-filter
term, and Seg-ARE degrades to plain truncation
(Section~\ref{sec:truncation}). The empirical wins, ties, and losses
across the four SOSD distributions are discussed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.
