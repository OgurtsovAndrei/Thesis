\section{Benchmark Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

Real-world keys come from the four SOSD datasets described in
Section~\ref{sec:sosd} (Facebook, Wiki, Books, OSM) and are
loaded in their native bit-width.
Synthetic distributions supplement them: uniform, spread, zipfian,
and temporal generators mask their output to $60$ bits, while the
clustered generator emits full $64$-bit keys (cluster centers and
the uniform fraction are drawn from the whole \texttt{uint64} range).
The only exception to native-width handling is SNARF: its upstream
C++ implementation cannot accept keys close to $\texttt{UINT64\_MAX}$,
so the SNARF wrapper masks inputs to $60$~bits internally. All other
filters (Grafite, SuRF, Rosetta, and our ARE variants) operate on the
full provided width.

Competing implementations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seg-ARE}: our simplified 1D DBSCAN hybrid filter (Section~\ref{sec:hybrid-scan}).
    \item \textbf{Grafite}~\citep{grafite2024}: information-theoretically optimal
    range filter (SIGMOD~2024).
    \item \textbf{SNARF}~\citep{vaidya2022snarf}: learned CDF-based range filter.
    \item \textbf{SuRF}~\citep{zhang2018surf}: succinct range filter with three variants
    (base, hash suffix, real suffix).
    \item \textbf{Rosetta}~\citep{luo2020rosetta}: Bloom-hierarchy range filter (SIGMOD~2020).
\end{itemize}

All filters are evaluated on FPR-vs-BPK tradeoff curves, measured over $10^6$
random empty-range queries per configuration --- enough to register
$10^3$--$10^4$ false positives at our target FPR range of $10^{-2}$--$10^{-3}$
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-workload-params}, ``\nameref{par:fpr-targets}'').
The query workload follows Section~\ref{sec:query-distribution}:
FPR is measured on the empty-only stream
(Section~\ref{subsec:queries-empty}), latency on the combined
stream (Section~\ref{subsec:queries-combined}). The latency tables
of Section~\ref{sec:eval-tables} also report a small follow-up
comparison between the two workloads.
